Research

My research examines the unintended consequences of financial reporting regulation: how managers respond to new rules, and how those responses can diverge from what standard setters intended.

Research Agenda

My research interests lie in financial accounting and reporting, with a particular focus on the unintended consequences of regulatory changes. Regulators revise reporting requirements to improve information usefulness or reduce compliance costs. But because managers respond strategically to the incentives created by new rules, the realized effects of regulation may extend beyond, or diverge from, its stated objectives.

My dissertation studies the SEC's 2020 modernization of Regulation S-K, which gave managers greater discretion over MD&A. Using a semantic measure of disclosure uniformity built from sentence-transformer embeddings, I find that MD&A became significantly more uniform after the reform, so a change meant to encourage tailored disclosure instead pushed firms toward standardization. In coauthored work I study ASU 2017-04, which simplified the goodwill impairment test. Investors respond less strongly to impairment announcements after the standard and analysts' forecasts become less accurate, while the intended reductions in audit fees and reporting delays do not appear. Related work with coauthors on replication in accounting has shaped how I think about research credibility, and I plan to keep building an agenda on how reporting regulation produces consequences beyond those standard setters anticipated.
