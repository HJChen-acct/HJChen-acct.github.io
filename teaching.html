Teaching

My goal as an accounting instructor is to help students develop technical competence and the judgment to keep learning as the field changes.

Teaching Philosophy

Completing a calculation is not the end point. A student who finishes my course should be able to explain how an accounting number was constructed and whether it tells a decision-maker anything useful.

Accounting is a technical discipline, but it is also a way of reasoning about business activity, and I teach it as both. Students learn it most deeply when they grasp the economic logic behind a procedure, because memorized steps collapse the moment a problem changes shape. Computing an overhead rate is not the finish line, so I push students to ask why the company chose that cost driver and what reported profitability would look like under a different method. Working through this shows them that accounting numbers rest on estimates and judgment calls, and that a technically correct calculation can still mislead when stripped of its context.

Generative AI has made the gap between producing an answer and understanding one impossible to ignore. Rather than prohibit it, I build it into activities that make students' reasoning visible: they share the prompts they use and evaluate the responses they get back, comparing the model's reasoning with their own before judging whether its conclusion fits the facts of the problem. The same habit of verification applies to a financial statement. Students also arrive with different levels of preparation, and I treat high standards and individualized support as complementary, asking students in office hours to explain their approach so I can find where their reasoning first diverged from the accounting logic.

I have come to see teaching and research as the same activity pointed at different audiences. What I want students to carry out of my courses is command of the fundamentals and the judgment to question any conclusion that sounds more certain than the evidence behind it.
